# Question Answering and the Machine Reading Landscape

## Motivation

Question answering (QA) is the task on which most of modern NLP was pressure-tested. SQuAD (Rajpurkar et al., 2016) turned "reading comprehension" from a curiosity into a benchmark that BERT, RoBERTa, and every subsequent encoder had to clear before it could be taken seriously. Today QA also sits underneath every user-facing product built on top of a large language model — support agents, search boxes with a natural-language layer, "chat with your docs", legal and medical assistants — so an NLP engineer will meet the same formulations under many different names.

This module owns the *reader* and *generator* side of QA: given a question and some evidence, produce an answer of the right shape, with the right calibration, and with the right evaluation. Retrieval — the *retriever* side — is deep enough to deserve its own track (`rag-engineer`); we mark that boundary clearly and refuse to redo it here.

## A working taxonomy

QA systems differ along three orthogonal axes, and confusing them is the number-one source of "we built the wrong thing" incidents.

| Axis                        | Options                                                                                                | What it decides                                                                                              |
|-----------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Evidence source**         | Open-book (context supplied) · Closed-book (model must know) · Retrieval-augmented (model fetches context) | Whether the model needs a retriever, and where hallucinations come from.                                     |
| **Answer form**             | Extractive span · Abstractive free-form text · Multiple-choice · Yes / no · Numeric                     | Whether the head is a span classifier, a seq2seq decoder, a classifier, or a constrained decoder.            |
| **Question type**           | Single-passage factoid · Multi-hop over passages · Conversational (context carries) · Non-factoid ("how do I…") | Evaluation strategy, need for evidence tracking, and whether reference answers even exist.                   |

A concrete production system usually pins each axis. "Extractive, open-book, single-passage factoid" is the SQuAD 1.1 recipe. "Abstractive, retrieval-augmented, non-factoid" is a modern documentation assistant. "Closed-book, decoder-only, multi-hop" is a bare LLM asked to answer without tools — and the failure mode most likely to hallucinate.

## The four QA formulations we cover

1. **Extractive QA.** The answer is a *contiguous span* of a supplied context (usually a Wikipedia paragraph or document chunk). A transformer encoder predicts two probability distributions over token positions — start and end — and the highest-scoring valid span wins. This is the SQuAD formulation and the workhorse for information-extraction-style QA where you need a citation into the source text. Covered in chapters 02–04.
2. **Abstractive QA (encoder-decoder).** The answer is generated as free-form text conditioned on a supplied context. Trained on datasets like NarrativeQA (Kočiský et al., 2018) or MS MARCO Question Answering (Nguyen et al., 2016). Uses T5, BART, FLAN-T5, or a modern instruction-tuned encoder-decoder. Covered in chapter 05.
3. **Closed-book QA (decoder-only).** The answer is generated by a decoder-only LLM from *its parameters alone* — no context supplied at inference. Trained implicitly during pretraining; probed by benchmarks like TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017) and Natural Questions (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019) in the closed-book setting studied by Roberts, Raffel & Shazeer (2020). Covered in chapter 06.
4. **Retrieval-augmented QA.** The system first retrieves relevant passages, then a reader/generator produces the answer conditioned on them. Introduced as RAG by Lewis et al. (2020). We build the *reader/generator* piece; the retriever piece lives in the `rag-engineer` track (chapter 10).

## Open-book vs. closed-book vs. retrieval-augmented — the practical difference

The three settings differ in *where the knowledge lives*, and therefore in *where the failure surface is*:

- **Open-book / extractive.** Knowledge is in the supplied context. Failure mode: the model picks the wrong span or is beaten by a distractor sentence. Fixed by better reading, not more knowledge.
- **Closed-book / parametric.** Knowledge is in the weights. Failure mode: outdated facts, low-frequency entities, plausible-but-wrong hallucinations. Fixed by retraining, distillation from a stronger model, or — usually — by moving to retrieval-augmented.
- **Retrieval-augmented.** Knowledge is in the corpus you retrieve over. Failure mode: retriever misses, the reader stitches together the wrong two passages, or the generator ignores the retrieved context and hallucinates. Fixed by improving the retriever *and* by teaching the reader to abstain when retrieval is empty.

A useful diagnostic: if you can imagine a user asking a follow-up "where did you get that from?", you almost certainly need extractive or retrieval-augmented QA, not closed-book. If the user does not care about provenance and does care about fluency, closed-book or abstractive is on the table.

## The QA / RAG boundary — briefly, up front

This module ends with a chapter (10) that draws the boundary in detail, but it is worth being explicit now:

- **NLP-engineer scope (this module):** the reader and the generator. Span heads, seq2seq decoders, decoder-only prompting, span-vs-null calibration, multi-hop decomposition, long-context reading, and answer evaluation.
- **RAG-engineer scope (the `rag-engineer` track):** the retrieval side. Chunking strategies, dense vs. sparse indexes, hybrid retrieval, reranking, citation formatting, index freshness, evaluation of retrieval quality (recall\@k, MRR, nDCG).

Both roles co-own the *interface* between retriever and reader: schema of retrieved passages, how the reader handles zero-hit retrieval, and how end-to-end metrics attribute failures. Everything else on the retrieval side belongs to the other track and we will link out rather than re-derive it.

## Where reference answers stop being enough

The classical SQuAD-era evaluation — exact match and F1 against a small set of reference strings — was designed for short, extractive answers with a well-defined gold span. It is *the* right tool for chapters 02–04, and we will teach it carefully.

But it degrades quickly outside that setting:

- Abstractive answers can be correct in infinitely many surface forms. String overlap penalises paraphrase.
- Multi-hop answers may be correct while the *reasoning chain* is wrong; only chain-annotated benchmarks catch this.
- Non-factoid "how do I…" answers have no single right answer at all.
- Long-form answers require judging faithfulness to a context that itself may be imperfect.

Chapter 11 therefore covers **human-evaluation rubrics** for QA: how to define correctness, faithfulness, completeness, and citation quality when reference strings are not enough — and how to use LLM-as-judge safely as an approximation.

## Chapter summary

- QA is the task on which reading, generation, and reasoning are all pressure-tested together. Every production LLM feature reduces to a variant of QA.
- Pin three axes before you start: evidence source, answer form, question type. Most production incidents are somebody solving one axis when the product needed a different one.
- We cover four formulations: extractive on spans, abstractive with encoder-decoder, closed-book with decoder-only, and retrieval-augmented (reader side only).
- The QA / RAG boundary is real: we own the reader/generator; the retriever belongs to the `rag-engineer` track. Chapter 10 makes the hand-off contract explicit.
- Reference-based metrics (EM, F1) are correct for SQuAD-style extractive QA and misleading almost everywhere else. Chapter 11 replaces them with rubrics for the rest.
